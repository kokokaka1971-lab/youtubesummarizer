Generate notes For students

Generated notes are genuinely excellent at one job and actively counterproductive at another. The two look identical from the outside, which is why so many people adopt them for the wrong one.

Worth separating them before you build a workflow on top, because the difference decides whether the tool helps you or just makes you feel prepared.

What generated notes are good at

They are a map. A ninety-minute recording becomes a labelled structure you can navigate — sections, claims, and the timestamp for each.

That solves a real problem. Finding the four minutes you actually need in a long recording is otherwise a scrubbing exercise, and scrubbing is how people end up rewatching the whole thing.

They are also good at capture you would not otherwise do. Nobody takes careful notes on the third conference talk of the afternoon. Generated notes mean that talk is still searchable next month.

What they are bad at

Producing understanding. Reading a well-organised set of notes creates a strong sense of having learned the material — the same sense watching the video created, arriving faster.

Mueller and Oppenheimer's much-cited study, reported by the Association for Psychological Science, found students who transcribed lectures verbatim did worse on conceptual questions than those who had to reframe ideas in their own words. Later replication work has complicated the detail, but the mechanism it points at is the durable part: reframing is where the processing happens.

Generated notes are the logical endpoint of verbatim capture: perfect, complete, and produced entirely without you. If they replace the step where you put the idea in your own words, they have removed the part that was doing the work.

This is easy to state and easy to drift away from, because the wrong use is so much more comfortable. Reading a tidy set of notes feels like progress in a way that staring at a blank page trying to remember the second constraint does not. The blank page is the part that works.

The workflow that uses both

The ordering below is the part people get wrong. Generating first and recalling second feels more efficient and removes the entire benefit, because once you have read the structure you can no longer discover what you failed to reconstruct.

The version that works treats generated notes as the reference and your own as the learning. Four steps, about fifteen minutes per video.

  1. Watch once without taking notes. Splitting attention between understanding and transcribing means transcribing usually wins.
  2. Write the outline from memory. Uncomfortable, and that is the point — the discomfort is what shows you where the gaps are.
  3. Generate the notes and compare. The difference between the two documents is your gap list, with a timestamp attached to each gap.
  4. Rewatch only those minutes, then write each gap in your own words underneath.

We set out the reasoning behind this at length in how to actually study from YouTube.

What the notes contain

Four depths, from a single line to full structured notes, so you can match the output to the job rather than always getting the same document — all four are listed on the features page.

  • Sections rebuilt from the content, not from uploader chapters.
  • Supporting detail under each claim, not just headings.
  • A clickable timestamp on every point, so nothing is unverifiable.
  • Markdown export that keeps the links intact.

Markdown matters more than it sounds. Notes that lose their timestamps on the way into your app become unverifiable exactly when you have forgotten enough to need checking.

It is worth exporting immediately rather than later. Without an account there is no history to come back to, and the most common way people lose a summary is closing the tab intending to save it afterwards.

Some videos take notes better than others

This is worth checking before you commit to a workflow, because the variation is larger than people expect. The same tool can produce excellent notes for one video and near-useless notes for the next.

The format of the source changes what you get back, and it is worth setting expectations before you run a batch.

Structured talks — lectures, conference sessions, explainers — produce the best notes, because the speaker had an outline and the transcript carries it. Interviews produce decent topic maps but weaker notes, since the structure is associative rather than planned.

Demonstrations are the weak case. A tutorial where the important part is what is happening on screen gives you a transcript of someone saying "and then we click here", which summarises into nothing useful. That is a property of the video, not the tool.

Where this fits for different people

Students use it to build a gap list before revision. Researchers use it to triage a conference playlist and cite the exact second of a claim. Teams use it so one person watches the two-hour recording and everyone else reads four hundred words.

A note on what to keep

Generated notes are cheap to produce, which makes it tempting to keep all of them. Resist that: a folder of two hundred auto-generated documents is an archive nobody searches.

The ones worth keeping are the ones you annotated — where you added your own explanation of the part that did not land. Those are the notes that will mean something to you in three months, because they contain something you produced rather than something you received.

For everything else, the video is still there and the summary takes thirty seconds to regenerate. Storage is not the constraint; finding things later is.

Frequently asked questions

Can I export the notes to Notion or Obsidian?

Export is Markdown, which both accept directly. Timestamp links survive the move, so notes stay checkable after they leave the page.

How detailed are the notes?

You choose from four depths. The deepest covers each section with its supporting detail; the shortest is a single line for deciding whether the video is worth your time.

Will generated notes help me remember the material?

On their own, not much — reading notes produces familiarity rather than recall. They help a great deal at telling you what to practise recalling, which is a different and genuinely useful job.

Do I need an account to generate notes?

No. Five videos a day at every depth, with export included. An account only adds history that follows you between devices.

Can I generate notes for several videos at once?

One video at a time, up to five a day on the free tier. For a playlist, work through it in order and export as you go — there is no history without an account, so anything unexported is gone when the tab closes.
