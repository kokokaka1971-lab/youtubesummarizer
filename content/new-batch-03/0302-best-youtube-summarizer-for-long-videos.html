Best YouTube Summarizer for Long Videos

Long videos are where summarizers separate, and where almost none of them publish a straight answer about their limits. We make one of these tools; here is what is actually documented, and the test that settles it in two seconds.

Summarize a long video Long video guide

Every tool in this category handles a twenty-minute explainer. The interesting question starts around ninety minutes and becomes decisive past two hours.

What makes it hard to shop for is that length limits are rarely published. You discover them by hitting them — or worse, by not noticing you have.

The failure mode is silence, not an error

A tool that refuses a four-hour video has told you something true. You go elsewhere and lose a minute.

A tool that accepts it and returns a confident summary of the first forty minutes has done something worse. The output reads as complete, every sentence in it is accurate about the part that was read, and nothing marks the hour that went missing.

There is a documented case of a long-video summary jumping from the 46-minute mark straight to 2:44:00, with everything between absent from the output and no indication given.

Why length breaks things

Three limits bite as a video grows, and they fail differently.

Fetch limits cap how much transcript is retrieved. Context limits cap how much a model reads at once. Attention is the subtle one: even when everything fits, research on long-context models found performance degrades when the relevant information sits in the middle rather than near either end.

That third one explains the pattern people report constantly — strong opening, strong conclusion, vague middle. The research published as Lost in the Middle found exactly this degradation, and it is not fixed by a bigger context window alone.

The two-second test

Whatever tool you are evaluating, this settles it faster than any review, and it is the first of the seven tests we would run on any tool.

  1. Compare the last timestamp with the runtime. A 2:50:00 video whose final citation is 0:52:00 was read to a third of its length.
  2. Check the spread. Evenly distributed suggests the whole thing was processed; clustered at the front means the tail was skimmed.
  3. Ask about the ending. Pick something specific from the last fifteen minutes and see whether the summary accounts for it.

Do this before reading the summary rather than after. Once you have formed an impression from something incomplete, it is hard to discard — you will remember the video as being about whatever the first third covered.

Timestamps are the feature that matters here

On short videos, timestamps are a convenience. On a three-hour replay they are the only mechanism that makes an incomplete read visible.

A summary without them can be a summary of the first twenty minutes and you will never know. That is why we would rank timestamp support above almost everything else for this use case, and it is the reason we treat them as structural rather than decorative.

Glasp publishes clickable timestamped summaries. NotebookLM works from the transcript and keeps sources citable. Both give you something to check against, which is more than a plain prose summary offers.

Why the summary should not get longer

People expect a three-hour video to produce a longer summary and treat a short one as a failure. It is the opposite.

A summary answers a fixed question: what did this argue, and where. Three hours does not contain three times the argument of one hour — it contains the same kind of argument with more examples, more setup and more digression around it.

What should grow with runtime is the chapter map, because there are genuinely more sections to navigate. If the takeaway list grows in proportion to the input, nothing was compressed.

Which long videos are worth it at all

Length alone does not make a good candidate — what matters is how unevenly the value is spread.

A three-hour interview where twenty minutes matter is ideal. A three-hour cumulative workshop is close to the worst case, because there is no part you can safely skip and a map does not help you skip it.

The quick sort: would someone who watched only the last fifteen minutes understand it? If yes, summarising serves you well. If no, the video wants watching and the summary is a revision aid afterwards.

Where we fall short

We have no browser extension, so summaries do not appear inside the YouTube page the way Glasp's do. We generate no mind maps or flashcards, which NoteGPT publishes. And we cannot summarize a video with no caption track — we say so rather than guessing, which is right but is still a no.

On long videos specifically, what we would claim is no length tier, overlapping passes so the middle is not treated as leftovers, and timestamps spread across the runtime so a gap is visible at a glance.

Test that claim rather than taking it. The last-timestamp check works on us exactly as it works on anyone else.

What to take from this

The category has no published answer to its own hardest question, which means you cannot shop for length handling — you have to test it.

The good news is that testing takes two seconds and works on every tool equally. Summarize your longest real video, look at the final timestamp, compare it with the runtime. That single number tells you more than any comparison page, including this one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the longest video these tools can handle?

Mostly unpublished, which is the problem. Rather than trusting a stated limit, run your longest real video through and compare the final timestamp with the runtime.

Why do long summaries miss the middle?

Either the transcript was truncated to fit, or it was read whole but attended to unevenly — long-context research finds accuracy drops for information sitting in the middle of a long input.

Does a longer video cost more?

Here it counts as one of your five daily free summaries regardless of length. Quota-based tools like NoteGPT price by usage, so check how a long video is counted before relying on it.

Do any of them handle livestream replays?

Anything that reads a caption track can, once captions exist after the stream ends. That can take a while on a long replay — see summarizing livestreams.

Should I just split the video up?

You should not have to, and doing it manually costs more than it saves. A chapter map over the full runtime gives you the same navigability without clipping anything.
