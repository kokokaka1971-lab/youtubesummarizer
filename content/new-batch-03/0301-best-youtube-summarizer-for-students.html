Student needs are specific enough that the general "best summarizer" answer is usually wrong. You are dealing with long recordings, technical vocabulary, a budget of roughly nothing, and a deadline.

Those four constraints rule things in and out quickly, so it is worth being concrete about what each tool actually commits to.

The caption requirement nobody mentions

This catches students more than anyone, because lecture recordings are exactly the content most likely to lack captions.

Google's documentation is explicit that NotebookLM supports only public YouTube videos with captions, that it imports only the text transcript, that videos without speech are unsupported, and that videos uploaded within the previous 72 hours may not be importable.

That last one matters in term time. A lecture posted this morning may not be summarisable this afternoon — by any tool, since they all depend on the same caption track existing.

Study outputs are where we are genuinely behind

If flashcards and mind maps are what you want, we do not have them and NoteGPT does — its published feature list includes mind maps, flashcards and auto-quizzes alongside summaries.

That is a real gap, and it is worth being direct about it rather than arguing the features do not matter. For spaced-repetition revision, generated flashcards are a legitimate workflow.

What we would say is that the flashcards are only as good as your judgement about which ideas deserve cards, and generating them automatically skips that judgement — which is the same argument we make about generated notes generally.

Cost, realistically

A student budget is not a rhetorical device. Per-month subscriptions across five modules add up quickly, and most tools are priced for professionals.

NotebookLM is free with a Google account. Glasp publishes its YouTube summary service as free. NoteGPT runs a limited free tier with paid plans from $9/month. We give five summaries a day with no account at all.

Five a day is the number worth interrogating for your own use. If you are mapping one lecture per weekday it is ample; during a revision week working through a semester of recordings, it is not, and you should plan for that rather than discover it.

What actually helps you pass

Worth separating the feature comparison from the thing that determines your grade, because they are not the same.

No summarizer improves recall. Reading a clean set of generated notes produces the same false fluency as rewatching the lecture, arriving faster. The tool's job is to tell you what to practise retrieving, not to do the retrieving.

Which means the feature that matters most is the one that makes a gap findable: a chapter map with timestamps, so "I lost the thread at the second constraint" becomes a specific minute. The method is set out in how to actually study from YouTube.

The deadline problem nobody designs for

Student usage is not evenly spread. You summarize nothing for three weeks, then need fifteen lectures mapped in two days before an exam.

That pattern punishes daily caps specifically. Five a day is generous across a normal term and useless in the week that matters, and the same is true of any volume-limited free tier including ours.

The fix is not a better tool, it is a different habit: run each lecture once in the week it is posted rather than batching them at the end. A map built in week three is a revision asset in week eleven; fifteen maps built the night before are just more reading you have not done.

How to choose in ten minutes

Take the longest, most technical lecture on your course and run it through two or three candidates, using the seven tests we would run on any tool. You are checking four things.

  1. Coverage. Does the last timestamp reach the end of the recording?
  2. Vocabulary. Are the technical terms transcribed correctly, or quietly mangled?
  3. Timestamps. Click three — do they land on the claim or near it?
  4. Export. Can the output reach your notes app with links intact?

Number two is the student-specific one. Automatic captioning is least reliable on exactly the terminology your exam is about, and no summarizer recovers a word the transcription never got right.

Where we would not recommend us

If you want flashcards or mind maps generated for you, NoteGPT publishes both and we publish neither. If you want summaries appearing inside the YouTube page as you browse, Glasp's extension does that and we have no extension at all.

If your material is not on YouTube — Panopto, Echo360, Blackboard — none of this helps, including us. Lecture capture platforms are outside what any of these tools read.

What we would claim is coverage of long recordings without silent truncation, timestamps carried through rather than reattached afterwards, and an honest failure when a recording has no captions. For revision built on finding your gaps, that is the part that matters.

Frequently asked questions

Which YouTube summarizer is free for students?

NotebookLM is free with a Google account, Glasp publishes its summary service as free, and we give five a day with no account. NoteGPT runs a limited free tier with paid plans from $9/month.

Will it work on my university's lecture recordings?

Only if they are on YouTube, including unlisted uploads. Panopto, Echo360 and similar capture platforms are not supported by any of these tools.

Can I get flashcards from a lecture video?

Not from us — we do not generate flashcards. NoteGPT's published feature list includes flashcards, mind maps and quizzes, so that is the direction to look if this is your priority.

What if the lecture has no captions?

Nothing works. Every tool here reads the caption track, and Google documents the same requirement for NotebookLM. For a recording posted in the last few hours, captions may simply not have generated yet.

Is it accurate enough to revise from?

Accurate enough to find things, not to replace understanding. Check technical terms at the timestamp, and use the summary to locate your gaps rather than as the thing you revise from.
