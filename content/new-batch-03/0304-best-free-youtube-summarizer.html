Four ways "free" gets limited

Knowing which shape a tool uses tells you whether it fits how you actually work, which a price of zero does not.

The US Federal Trade Commission's Guide Concerning Use of the Word "Free" is direct that conditions attached to a free offer must be stated clearly and conspicuously with the offer. A good deal of this category would struggle against that standard.

The limits nobody publishes

Everything above concerns limits a vendor chose to state. The more consequential ones tend to be the limits nobody mentions anywhere, and they surface only under the conditions you care about most.

Beyond the published figures, the seven tests we would run on any tool apply just as much to free tools as paid ones. Volume caps get advertised because they are easy to state. Length caps mostly do not, and they are the ones that bite.

A tool can be genuinely unlimited on count and quietly truncate anything past an hour. You will not see that on a pricing page, and the output will not mention it either — which is why the coverage check matters more than the pricing table.

Our own free tier states five a day up front for exactly this reason. Test with the longest video you actually care about, not a short demo clip, and compare the last timestamp against the runtime.

Why free tiers exist at all

This is worth a paragraph because the economics predict the behaviour, and the behaviour is what you will actually experience once you start depending on a tool.

Understanding the economics explains the patterns. Every summary costs the operator money — a transcript is fetched and a model reads it. A ninety-minute talk is roughly twelve thousand words of input for a few hundred words of output.

So a free tier is either a marketing cost with a hard ceiling, or it is subsidised by something else. Tools backed by a larger product, like NotebookLM, can be free in a way a standalone tool cannot.

That is worth knowing because it predicts stability. A free tier that is someone's acquisition budget can change next quarter; one that is a feature of a bigger platform usually will not.

What a good free tier looks like

  • The limit is stated up front, not discovered on your sixth video.
  • The output is not degraded — a worse summary for anonymous users is charging you in quality.
  • Export is included, because output that cannot leave is output you will retype.
  • Failure is honest — no captions should produce a message, not an invented summary.

The third one is a useful filter. A tool that shows you a summary but gates copying it has decided your work product is the paywall, which tells you how it will treat you later.

Free tiers and the tools that vanish

One risk rarely mentioned: tools in this category appear and disappear quickly, and a free tool with no revenue has no particular reason to still exist next year.

That matters if you are building a habit around one. Summaries you exported are yours; summaries stored in someone's web app are not, and a service shutting down takes them with it.

The practical protection is the same regardless of which tool you pick: export anything you want to keep, into a format you control. Markdown in your own notes is durable in a way a vendor's history page is not.

What to take from this

Free is easy to advertise and hard to compare, because the interesting constraint is usually the one nobody publishes.

Judge a free tier on three things: whether the limit is stated before you hit it, whether the anonymous output is the full product, and whether you can export. Those predict how a tool will treat you far better than the headline price, which is zero everywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Which YouTube summarizer is completely free?

Glasp publishes its YouTube summary service as free, and NotebookLM is free with a Google account. We give five a day with no account. "Completely free and unlimited" is rare, because each summary costs the operator money.

Are free summarizers worse than paid ones?

Not reliably. Price tracks business model more than quality — a paid tool can still truncate long videos, and a free one can carry timestamps properly. Test rather than reading the pricing page.

What's the catch with free tiers?

Usually an undisclosed length cap rather than the advertised volume cap. Check by summarizing your longest video and comparing the final timestamp with the runtime.

Do I need an account to use a free summarizer?

Depends on the tool. Glasp offers a no-sign-up web option, we require no account at all, and NotebookLM needs a Google account. Free and account-free are separate questions.

Will the free tier still exist next year?

Worth thinking about before building a workflow on one. A free tier funded as marketing can change; one that is part of a larger platform tends to be more stable.
