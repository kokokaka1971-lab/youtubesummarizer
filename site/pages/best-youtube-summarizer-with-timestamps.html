Best YouTube Summarizer With Timestamps

Nearly every tool advertises timestamps. Far fewer produce ones that land on the claim they are attached to, and the difference is invisible until you click — which is why it survives.

Try it with timestamps How they work

The short version: a summary you cannot check is something you either believe or ignore. Timestamps turn it into something you can use.

Two ways to build them, one of which drifts

Carried through. The transcript arrives as fragments with times attached, and those times travel with the text through every transformation — fragments into sentences, sentences into topics, topics into a claim.

Matched back. The text is summarised first, then times are attached by searching the transcript for words resembling the output and citing whatever matches best.

The second is far easier to build and works acceptably when the wording barely changed. It drifts precisely where the summary rewrote heavily — which is, without exception, the claims most worth checking.

The twenty-second test

This works on any tool, including ours, and tells you more than any feature grid. It is the second of the seven tests we would run on any tool.

  1. Find the most rewritten bullet — the one phrased least like anything a person would say aloud. Matching breaks there first.
  2. Click it. Within a few seconds of the claim is real. Right topic, half a minute early, is reconstruction.
  3. Try one near the end. Drift grows with distance into the video, so the final third exposes it worst.

Three clicks. If they land, the mapping is real and the rest of the tool is probably built with similar care.

The use almost nobody thinks of

Timestamps make an incomplete read visible, and that is arguably worth more than the navigation.

If a video runs two hours and the final citation sits at thirty-eight minutes, the tool read less than a third and wrote confidently about the whole. A plain summary hides that completely — there is no other signal.

So even if you never click a timestamp to navigate, having them lets you verify you are reading a summary of the video rather than of its opening. More on that in summarizing long videos.

Export is where timestamps quietly die

This is the step people discover too late, usually months after the fact, when the note they were relying on turns out to be unverifiable text.

A tool can produce perfect timestamps and still leave you with unverifiable notes, if the links do not survive leaving the page.

This is worth checking explicitly: export the summary, paste it into wherever your notes live, and see whether the times are still links. Plain-text times that no longer click are decoration.

It matters most for anything you hand to someone else. They cannot check what you cannot point at, and a claim without a locatable source is the thing this whole category is supposed to fix.

Chapters are not the same thing

Worth separating, because tools conflate them. Uploader chapters are markers a creator typed into the description — plenty of videos have none, and they describe sections rather than claims.

Search engines model this distinction too: schema.org's Clip type describes specific points in a video, each addressing a distinct topic. A timestamped summary starts from what was said, rebuilds the structure and anchors each claim to a second. It works whether or not the uploader bothered, which for most uploads means the difference between having navigation and not.

If a tool only surfaces existing chapters, it will look excellent on the minority of videos that have them and useless on everything else.

What timestamps do not fix

Worth being precise, because it is easy to oversell. Timestamps do not make a summary more accurate — the same clause still went missing during compression.

They change the cost of recovering it: one second instead of ninety minutes. That is a smaller claim than "our summaries are correct", and a far more useful one, because it is the only version of the claim you can actually check.

It also reframes what a summary is for. Without them it is a replacement for the video, which is a lot of trust to place in six bullets. With them it is an index into the video, which is modest and much more defensible.

Where we fall short

No browser extension, so unlike Glasp the summary does not appear inside the YouTube page while you browse — you paste a link into a web page instead. No mind maps or flashcards. No summary at all when a video lacks captions.

What we hold to is that the timings travel through the pipeline rather than being reattached afterwards, that every depth carries them, and that Markdown export keeps them as links.

Which is a claim you should test rather than accept. Three clicks settles it.

What to take from this

Every tool in this comparison says it provides timestamps. None publishes how they are produced, and that is the only part that decides whether they are worth anything.

So the feature checkbox is close to meaningless and the three-click test is not. Twenty seconds on a video you already know settles it, and it keeps working as tools change and this page goes stale.

Frequently asked questions

Which YouTube summarizers have clickable timestamps?

Glasp and NoteGPT both publish timestamped summaries you can jump from, and so do we. NotebookLM imports the transcript and keeps sources citable rather than offering time-linked summary points.

How do I know the timestamps are accurate?

Click three, including one from the last third of the video and one heavily-rewritten bullet. Landing within a few seconds means the timings were carried through; landing vaguely nearby means they were matched back afterwards.

Do timestamps work on videos with no chapters?

They should. Uploader chapters and generated timestamps are different things — a tool that only surfaces existing chapters will fail on the majority of videos, which have none.

Do the timestamps survive into my notes?

Markdown export keeps them as links here. Worth testing on any tool, because links that become plain text leave you with notes you cannot verify later.

Are timestamps worth paying for?

They are on the free tier here and on Glasp's free service, so paying specifically for them is rarely necessary. What varies is accuracy, which no pricing page tells you.
