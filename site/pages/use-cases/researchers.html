Reading is faster than watching, and always was

A conference talk runs at roughly 130 words a minute because that's the speed of speech. You read at three or four times that. The only reason to sit through a recorded talk at speaking pace is that there was no text version — which is a technical problem, not a fact of nature.

The workflow that follows is the one that falls out of fixing that: read everything at reading speed, then spend your watching budget deliberately. A YouTube video summarizer is just the mechanism — the discipline is deciding what earns the full forty minutes.

  1. 1

    Batch the playlist

    On Pro, run the whole playlist at once rather than pasting forty links. You get a TL;DR for each — about a paragraph, enough to tell relevance from adjacency.

  2. 2

    Shortlist on the takeaways

    For anything that survives the TL;DR, read the key takeaways. This is where most of the false positives die: talks whose title promised your problem and whose content addressed a neighbouring one.

  3. 3

    Watch the shortlist properly

    Four talks, watched attentively, with the chapter map open so you know what's coming. This is the part that was always worth your time — it just no longer costs you the other thirty-six.

  4. 4

    Cite to the second

    When you quote a talk, the timestamp goes in the citation. Anyone checking your work lands on the exact moment rather than being told to watch a forty-minute video and trust you.

Where you still have to be careful

A summary reflects what was said, not whether it holds up. Auto-generated captions also mis-hear specialist vocabulary and unfamiliar names more than they mis-hear ordinary speech, which is exactly the vocabulary you care about. Before anything goes in a citation, open the timestamp and check the wording against the source.
