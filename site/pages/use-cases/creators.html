The gap is the whole job

Most topics already have twenty videos. The useful question isn't "what is this topic about" — it's "what does every one of these twenty videos skip?" That gap is where your video justifies its existence, and finding it means holding all twenty in your head at once.

Twenty summaries side by side make that pattern obvious in a way twenty afternoons of watching never does, because by video fourteen you've forgotten what video three actually claimed. Running the field through a YouTube summarizer first means you hold all of it at once.

  1. 1

    Scan the top of the search results

    Take the videos that already rank for your topic and summarize the lot. Read the takeaways back to back and the consensus shows up immediately — as does the thing all of them assume without explaining.

  2. 2

    Steal structure, not sentences

    The chapter maps show you how the best performers actually pace a topic: how long they spend before the first payoff, where they put the caveat, what they cut. That's the transferable part — the wording isn't, and shouldn't be.

  3. 3

    Write to the gap

    You now know the twenty things your audience has already heard. Spend your runtime on the one they haven't, and open by acknowledging the rest rather than repeating it.

  4. 4

    Turn your own upload into show notes

    After publishing, run your own video through it. The chapter list becomes your description timestamps, the takeaways become the pinned comment, and the full notes become the blog version — all from the video you already made.

The obvious caveat

Research means understanding what's been said so you can add to it. Running someone's video through a summarizer and reading their script back as your own is plagiarism with extra steps, and it makes worse videos besides. Use the summaries to find the gap — then go and actually fill it.
