Timestamped YouTube Summary

A timestamped summary is not a summary with extra decoration. It is a different kind of object — one you can check, cite and navigate, rather than one you have to take on faith.

Get a timestamped summary How timestamps work

Two summaries of the same video can contain word-for-word identical sentences and still be worth completely different amounts, depending on one thing: whether you can get back to the moment each sentence came from, or whether you simply have to accept it.

This page is about what that changes in practice, and how to tell whether the timestamps you are looking at are real.

What the timestamps are for

Three jobs, and most people only ever use the first — which is a shame, because it is comfortably the least valuable of the three and the other two are what justify insisting on timestamps in the first place.

Navigation. Jump to the section you need without scrubbing. This is the obvious one and the least valuable.

Verification. Check a specific claim in ten seconds instead of rewatching. This is where the actual value sits.

Citation. Covered in more depth in the timestamps guide — point someone else at the exact moment — in a message, a document, an essay. A claim with a second attached is evidence; the same claim without one is hearsay with formatting.

Real timestamps versus reconstructed ones

There are two ways to produce a timestamped summary, and they look identical until you click.

Carried through. The transcript arrives as fragments with times attached, and those times travel with the text through every transformation — fragments into sentences, sentences into topics, topics into a claim.

Matched back. The text is summarised first and times are attached afterwards, by searching the transcript for words resembling the output and citing whatever matches best.

The second is much easier to build and works fine when the wording barely changed. It drifts exactly where the summary rewrote heavily — which is to say, on the claims most worth checking.

How to tell which you have

  1. Pick the most rewritten bullet. The one phrased least like anything a person would say aloud. Matching breaks there first.
  2. Click it. Landing within a few seconds of the claim is real. Landing in roughly the right topic, half a minute early, is reconstruction.
  3. Try one near the end. Drift tends to grow with distance into the video, so the last third is where reconstruction shows worst.

Three clicks, about twenty seconds, and it tells you more about a tool than any feature list will.

Timestamps make an incomplete read visible

This is the use almost nobody thinks of, and it is the reason to look at the timestamps before reading the summary.

If a video runs two hours and the final citation is at thirty-eight minutes, the tool read less than a third of it and wrote confidently about the whole. Nothing else in the output would have told you.

A plain summary hides that failure completely. That alone makes timestamps worth insisting on, even if you never use them to navigate — more on this in summarizing long videos.

Chapters, key moments and summaries

Worth distinguishing three things that all involve times.

Uploader chapters are markers a creator typed into the description. Plenty of videos have none, and they describe sections rather than claims.

Key moments are what search engines surface — specific points each addressing a distinct topic, described in schema.org's Clip type and used to let a viewer jump straight to the relevant part.

A timestamped summary starts from what was said, rebuilds the structure, and anchors each claim. It works whether or not the uploader bothered with chapters, which most did not.

What timestamps do not fix

Worth being precise, because it is easy to oversell this. Timestamps do not make a summary more accurate. The same clause still went missing during compression.

What they change is the cost of recovering it: one second instead of ninety minutes. That is the whole argument, and it is a smaller claim than "our summaries are correct" — but a much more useful one, because it is checkable.

There is a reasonable objection here. If you still have to verify, what did the summary buy you? The answer is that it moved the expensive part. Without one, you watch ninety minutes to find the four claims worth having. With one, you read six bullets, pick the one you will act on, and spend ten seconds confirming it.

The verification did not disappear. It narrowed from the whole video to a single sentence, which is the only form of the problem anyone actually solves.

Keeping the timestamps after export

Notes lose their links more often than people expect, and the loss is silent. You discover it months later, when you want to check something and find plain text.

Markdown export preserves the links, so a summary pasted into your notes app stays checkable. If you are copying by hand out of a page, copy the links too — the claim without the second attached is the part that ages badly.

This matters most for anything you will hand to someone else. They cannot check what you cannot point at.

Where this leaves you

A timestamped summary is best understood as an index rather than a replacement. It tells you what the video contained and exactly where, and it invites you to check rather than asking you to trust.

That is a modest claim, and modest claims are the ones that hold up. A tool promising summaries so accurate you never need the source is promising something compression cannot deliver — and the promise is unfalsifiable, which is precisely the problem.

Frequently asked questions

Are the timestamps clickable?

Yes — each opens the video at that second. Checking a claim is a click rather than a scrub, which is the difference between a check people do and one they skip.

Does it work if the video has no chapters?

Yes. Uploader chapters are ignored entirely — structure and timings both come from the caption track, so a video with an empty description behaves the same as one with full chapters.

How precise are they?

They point at the moment the claim was made, because the timings travel with the text rather than being reattached by word-matching afterwards. Click three and judge for yourself — that test works on any tool.

Can I share a summary with the timestamps intact?

Markdown export keeps every link, so a summary pasted into a document or message stays checkable by whoever reads it next.

Do timestamps cost extra?

No. They are on every summary at every depth, including the free tier, because a summary without them is a different and much weaker product.
