How to Copy YouTube Transcript Text

Four clicks on desktop, and worth knowing. Also worth knowing what the copy leaves behind, because the thing you lose is usually the thing that made it useful.

Skip the copying Transcript guide

YouTube will show you a transcript for most videos, and copying it is straightforward once you know where it hides — though summarizing the video directly skips the step entirely. The interface has moved around over the years, which is why people assume it is not there.

On desktop

  1. Open the video and look below it, at the description box.
  2. Expand the description — click the "...more" area.
  3. Click "Show transcript", usually near the bottom of the expanded description.
  4. Select and copy from the panel that appears on the right.

There is a three-dot menu at the top of that panel with a "Toggle timestamps" option. Turning timestamps off before copying gives you clean prose; leaving them on gives you text you can still locate in the video.

On mobile

Worth setting expectations before you try: this is one of the places where the mobile app and the desktop site diverge most, and the app has moved the option around repeatedly over the past few years.

Harder, and frequently missing. The transcript option appears inconsistently in the mobile app, and when it does the copying experience is poor.

The practical workaround is to open the video in a mobile browser and request the desktop site, which usually restores the transcript panel. For anything more than a quick look, do it on a computer.

What you lose in the copy

Copying feels lossless — it is text, and you selected all of it. Three things disappear anyway, and the first one is the expensive one.

This is the part worth understanding before you build a habit on it.

The timings, usually. Most people toggle timestamps off because they clutter the text. That makes the copy unlocatable — nothing in it can be traced back to a moment in the video.

The structure, entirely. A transcript has no sections, no paragraphs and no speaker labels. The sections were real; they simply are not marked anywhere in the text.

The scale, immediately. A ninety-minute video is roughly twelve thousand words. That is a long magazine feature, unedited, with no headings and nothing to skim.

Why the copy is harder to read than you expect

People are routinely surprised by how unusable a copied transcript is, having assumed text would be easier than video.

Speech is not writing. A competent speaker transcribes as run-on fragments with false starts, repetitions for emphasis, and no sentence boundaries — perfectly clear when heard, close to unreadable on a page.

Skimming does not rescue it either, because skimming depends on topic sentences and paragraph shape, and a transcript has neither. You end up reading most of it or none of it, and in practice it is none.

When the raw transcript is the right answer

Three cases, and they are the ones where compression actively works against you.

You need exact wording. Quoting from a summary attributes words to a speaker who did not say them. For anything you will publish or cite, only the transcript will do.

You are searching. Checking whether a term appeared at all is a ctrl-F problem, and a summary has by design thrown away most of the words.

You are captioning or translating. You want the source rather than an interpretation of it.

The mistake worth avoiding

One workflow accounts for most of the disappointment people report with transcripts, and it is so common that it is worth naming explicitly rather than leaving as an implication.

Copying the transcript and pasting it into a chatbot is the commonest workflow and has two costs people rarely account for.

Long transcripts hit limits, and what gets dropped is not announced. And the timings died at the copy step, so nothing in the output can be traced back to the video — you have swapped a checkable summary for one you can only believe.

Pasting a video link into a tool that fetches the captions itself avoids both, because the timings never leave the pipeline. We set that out in how to summarize a YouTube transcript.

What you can do with it afterwards

Worth a brief note, because people copy transcripts intending to republish them and that is a different question from copying one to read.

Personal use — reading, searching, quoting with attribution — is ordinary. Republishing someone's full transcript is governed by copyright and by YouTube's Terms of Service, and a transcript is not public domain simply because the video is public.

For quoting in your own work, the usual rules apply: short, attributed, and accurate. The transcript is the right source for that precisely because it preserves the exact wording a summary would have paraphrased.

If there is no "Show transcript"

The option only appears when a caption track exists. If it is missing, one of four things is true: the upload is too recent for captions to have generated, the creator disabled them, the video has no speech, or the language is unsupported.

Only the first resolves itself — try again in a few hours. The rest mean there is no text to copy, and no tool can produce one from the video's title.

The shortest advice

Copy the transcript when you need the exact words. Do not copy it as a step on the way to summarizing it somewhere else.

That second workflow is extremely common and quietly costs you the timings, which were the only thing making the result checkable. Paste the link rather than the text, and the problem does not arise.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the transcript button on YouTube?

Below the video, inside the description. Expand the description with "...more" and look for "Show transcript" near the bottom. It only appears when captions exist.

Can I copy a transcript on my phone?

Inconsistently in the app. Opening the video in a mobile browser and requesting the desktop site usually restores the transcript panel.

How do I remove the timestamps?

The three-dot menu at the top of the transcript panel has a "Toggle timestamps" option. Worth thinking twice — without them, nothing in the text can be located in the video again.

Should I copy the transcript or just summarize the video?

Copy it if you need exact wording, to search for a term, or to translate. Summarize if you want to know what the video argued and where — copying to then summarize elsewhere loses the timings.

Is it legal to copy a YouTube transcript?

Reading and copying for personal use is ordinary. Republishing someone's transcript is a different question governed by copyright and by YouTube's terms, and worth checking before you post one.

Or skip the copying entirely

Paste the video link instead of the transcript and keep the timings that make it checkable.
